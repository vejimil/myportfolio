01 Designing inside constraints

With limited phone use and network access, I wanted to make games that could still create shared fun: compact two-player games for one screen, quick to set up, understood in seconds.

The limitation became a design lens. Starting from classic game structures and simple forms, I explored what makes a small game feel exciting — pace, impact, variation, readability, and the sense that every input matters.

  • Easy to start and easy to read on a single screen
  • Familiar classic-game foundations, reshaped with new mechanics and tuning
  • Timing, feedback, and pacing to build stronger game feel
  • Iteration driven by how players reacted, not only by theory

02 Ping-Pong Smash

Ping-Pong Smash started from Pong, reinterpreted with a faster rhythm. Since many people now consume short, fast-paced media, a more immediate tempo felt right for short local sessions.

The key addition is a timed smash: hit it while the ball is approaching and rallies gain speed, tension, and physical satisfaction. It created moments of reaction, risk, and momentum shift — and I kept tuning the timing window and responsiveness through player feedback.

03 Space Duel

Space Duel began with a simpler question: how much game can grow from basic geometric shapes? I started with minimal forms and gradually built a combat loop around movement, shooting, health, items, and match variation.

As players reacted, I kept expanding the system — including a level-based bullet transformation, so rounds evolve over time instead of staying flat.

▶ Play Space Duel

What I learned

Fun rarely comes from one idea alone. It emerges through rhythm, readability, feedback, variation — and repeated tuning based on how people actually play.
