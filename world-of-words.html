World of Words

A vocabulary platform for beginners that turns word study into generated quizzes and replayable mini-games — designed to feel active, not like a digital word list.

01 The problem

This started with a frustration I kept running into while studying languages. Most vocabulary tools are useful, but they still feel like digital word lists: the same fixed question order, flat word–meaning pairs, and little sense of momentum.

They also create awkward translations when linguistically distant languages are forced into one direct pairing. So I treated World of Words as a system design problem: generate questions from word sets, choose more natural language pairings, and make review feel clearer and more replayable.

02 Design responses

  • Generated, not fixed. Questions are drawn randomly from vocabulary sets instead of repeating one sheet.
  • Exam-like where it helps. Japanese items can follow a JLPT-style format, so practice feels closer to real exam exposure.
  • Natural pairings. Korean–Japanese, English–French, and English–Spanish, instead of one source language for everything.
  • A learning loop, not an ending. Immediate feedback, a score screen, and a review of missed words keep momentum going.

03 Turning review into a game

The mini-games were inspired by typing games, but redesigned around meaning recognition instead of keyboard speed. I switched from typing to dragging or clicking for smoother play and less fatigue, so the challenge stays on comprehension, recognition speed, and attention.

The game section carries a stronger visual identity, and small characters like Nihongorae add warmth — while the product as a whole stays controlled enough to remain a practical study tool.

04 Built for spare moments

I expected people to use this in short bursts — commuting, waiting, filling a small gap in the day. So both the quiz and the games work on phones: hierarchy, tap-target size, readability, and feedback were all tuned for small screens, and the mini-games were re-laid out for vertical play.

05 Solo, with AI as a tool

I handled planning, design, development, and content structure myself. I used AI tools to speed up exploration and support implementation — but the direction, system design, content judgment, debugging, and final decisions were mine. The important part wasn't generating output; it was deciding what fit the product and fixing what didn't.

Why it matters to me

I built this during mandatory military service, in a limited environment and largely on my own. It became proof that I can keep learning under constraints and carry an idea far enough to make it real — and keep it live.
